# The 70/30 Rule: Why AI Needs an Experienced Pilot

There's a seductive narrative in tech right now: AI has democratized software development. Anyone can build an app. Describe what you want, the machine produces code. No experience necessary.

I've spent the last year testing this by building production Rails applications with AI assistance. Complete multi-tenant SaaS platforms with authentication, German tax compliance, and DATEV integration. Real software, real customers, real infrastructure.

The narrative is half right, which makes it dangerous.

AI genuinely transforms what's possible. I delivered a complete business application in 19 days that would have taken a traditional team four months. But the productivity gain doesn't come from AI replacing judgment. It comes from AI amplifying judgment. That distinction changes everything.

---

The no-code movement promised something similar a few years ago. Drag and drop your way to a business. For certain use cases, these tools deliver real value—forms, simple CRMs, basic inventory tracking. They've gotten genuinely good at that layer.

But then you need multi-tenancy, where each customer's data must be perfectly isolated. You need German invoicing with correct VAT calculations and reverse charge handling. You need background jobs that don't block requests. You need queries that don't collapse at fifty thousand records.

This is where no-code hits a wall. Not because the tools are bad, but because the problems require judgment that no visual interface can encode. The decisions depend on context that doesn't fit in a dropdown.

AI-assisted coding faces the same wall, just higher up. It generates vastly more sophisticated code. It understands frameworks, patterns, best practices. But it still can't make the judgment calls that determine whether software actually works in production.

---

Consider authentication. Ask AI to build it and you'll get something that works. Accounts, passwords, sessions. The code follows patterns correctly.

But will it handle the edge case where email delivery takes thirty seconds and blocks the request? Will it scope queries to the current tenant, or fetch users by ID directly—leaking data between customers? Will it use the hashing algorithm recommended today, or the one popular in 2022's training data?

These aren't exotic concerns. They're the ordinary problems that separate software that works from software that survives production.

---

I frame it as the 70/30 rule. AI handles about 70% of what I type—controller scaffolding, migrations, templates, tests, CSS. Patterns so well-represented in training data that AI produces them nearly perfectly.

But humans handle 100% of the decisions. Architecture: monolith or services, PostgreSQL or MySQL? Security: is this query scoped correctly, is this input sanitized? Business logic: does this match German tax requirements, does this workflow reflect what the client actually needs?

The 70% isn't 70% of the value. It's 70% of the typing. Value lives in the decisions.

---

There's an analogy I keep returning to. AI can generate architectural blueprints. Describe what you want—three bedrooms, open floor plan, modern kitchen—and you get professional-looking plans.

But no one hands those plans to someone off the street expecting a livable house. You still need builders who know the blueprints don't show where walls need extra support. Who recognize the plumbing will cause pressure problems. Who understand local building codes.

The blueprints compress weeks of architect time into hours. Valuable. But they're a starting point for skilled execution, not a replacement for skill.

Software works the same way. AI compresses mechanical coding into minutes instead of hours. That compression is real. But it's a starting point for experienced developers, not a substitute for experience.

---

What does this mean if you're trying to get software built?

The hype says anyone can build an app now. Technically true, practically useless. Anyone can generate code that looks like an app. Generating code that handles edge cases, scales under load, keeps data secure, and stays maintainable—that requires judgment AI doesn't have.

The realistic picture: experienced developers with AI assistance now deliver what used to require teams. The economics shifted dramatically. A project that needed €80,000 over four months might now need €20,000 over four weeks. That's a genuine transformation.

But the experienced developer part isn't optional. AI assistance only works because someone with judgment is at the controls.

For decision makers at larger companies evaluating how to build new products or internal tools: the question isn't whether to use AI-assisted development. That's already settled—the productivity gains are too significant to ignore. The question is who pilots the AI. The same tools that make a senior developer dramatically more productive can lead an inexperienced team into security holes and architectural dead ends.

For startups struggling to get started: you no longer need to raise money for a five-person team before you can validate your idea. One senior developer with AI assistance can build your MVP in weeks at a fraction of traditional costs. But "senior" isn't negotiable. The AI handles typing. Experience handles everything that matters.

For colleagues watching how the industry changes: the developers who thrive will be those who understand AI as an amplifier, not a replacement. Who use it for the 70% that's mechanical while focusing on the 30% that requires judgment. Who ship faster than those ignoring AI and better than those trusting it blindly.

---

DHH recently said he can "feel competence draining from his fingers" when he lets AI drive development. There's something important there.

Programming skill isn't knowing what code should look like. It's intuition built through thousands of hours of writing, debugging, and living with consequences. That intuition tells you something's wrong before you can articulate why. It makes you pause at an authorization check that passes tests but doesn't feel right.

AI can't have that intuition. Intuition comes from embodied experience. AI has patterns from other people's code. Useful for boilerplate. Useless for judgment.

The winning formula right now is clear: experienced developers using AI to eliminate mechanical work, focusing their time entirely on the decisions that determine success. That combination delivers what used to require teams, in timelines that used to seem impossible, at costs that make new categories of projects viable.

That's the 70/30 rule. AI handles the typing. Experience handles everything else.
