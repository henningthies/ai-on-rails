PICK THREE: HOW AI BROKE THE MAGIC TRIANGLE

Speed, cost, quality—the magic triangle said pick two. AI changed the math.

Every founder building software encounters the magic triangle eventually. Speed, cost, quality—pick two. Want it fast and cheap? Quality suffers. Fast and good? Expensive. Good and cheap? You'll wait forever.

This framework has governed software development for decades. It reflects a real constraint: software required human time at every step. More speed meant more developers working in parallel, which meant higher costs. Higher quality meant more careful work, which meant longer timelines. The dimensions were genuinely coupled because they all drew from the same finite resource—skilled human attention.

AI changed the equation by eliminating the mechanical bottleneck.

A substantial portion of traditional development work is typing. Boilerplate controllers, standard migrations, repetitive test patterns, routine view templates. This mechanical work doesn't require creativity or judgment—it just requires fingers on keyboards. And it used to require a lot of calendar time.

AI compresses that mechanical work dramatically. Code that took an afternoon to type now takes minutes to generate and review. The bottleneck shifts from production to judgment: deciding what to build, reviewing what AI produces, ensuring quality and security.

This compression breaks the coupling between output volume and team size. A single senior developer can now produce what previously required a small team—not by working harder, but by spending time on decisions rather than typing.

When one person can produce team-scale output, the triangle's constraints decouple.

Cost drops because you're paying one developer instead of five. The coordination overhead that adds 30-40% to team projects disappears entirely. Fixed-price engagements become feasible because scope is easier to control with a single point of accountability.

Speed increases because there's no coordination latency. No waiting for handoffs, no synchronization meetings, no revision cycles between team members. The person who understands the requirements writes the code and ships the code.

Quality improves because coherence is automatic. A single developer produces consistent patterns, unified architecture, uniform style. There are no interpretation gaps between team members, no conflicts between different technical visions, no telephone game degrading requirements into implementation.

Nothing is free. The approach only works with genuinely senior developers. AI amplifies skill rather than replacing it—a junior developer using AI produces mediocre code faster, while a senior developer produces excellent code faster. The quality dimension depends entirely on who's driving.

Scale eventually requires teams. One developer can build and maintain systems up to a certain complexity. For truly large platforms, you'll eventually need people. The question is whether that threshold comes during MVP development or later growth phases.

For most early-stage products, it comes later. The one-developer model delivers speed, cost, and quality benefits that more than compensate for its limitations. You can have it fast, affordable, and well-built—if you work with the right person.

The old triangle asked you to pick two. The new geometry lets you optimize all three.
