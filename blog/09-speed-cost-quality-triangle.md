# Pick Three: How AI Broke the Speed-Cost-Quality Triangle

Every founder building software has encountered the iron triangle: speed, cost, quality—pick two.

Want it fast and cheap? Quality suffers. Want it fast and good? It'll cost you. Want it good and cheap? You'll wait forever. This constraint has governed software development for decades, forcing painful trade-offs that shape company trajectories.

The triangle exists because software has traditionally required human time at every step. More speed meant more people working in parallel, which meant higher costs. Higher quality meant more careful work, which meant longer timelines. The constraint wasn't arbitrary—it reflected the fundamental economics of labor-intensive production.

AI changes those economics.

## The Old Triangle

In the traditional model, you built software by assembling teams. Backend developers, frontend specialists, DevOps engineers, project managers, designers—each person adding capability but also adding cost and coordination overhead.

The team model created structural constraints. Five developers cost five times as much as one, setting a floor on budget. Communication overhead scaled with team size, creating delays that extended timelines. Quality required review cycles and revision loops that consumed calendar time regardless of how many people you had.

These weren't failures of management or process. They were inherent properties of multi-person creative work. The more people involved, the more handoffs required, the more opportunities for miscommunication, the more time spent coordinating rather than building.

The triangle held because you couldn't escape these constraints. Speed required parallelization, which required people, which required money. Quality required coherence, which required communication, which required time. The dimensions were genuinely coupled.

## What AI Actually Changed

AI didn't eliminate the need for skilled developers. It eliminated the mechanical bottleneck that forced team-scale labor for team-scale output.

A substantial portion of traditional development work is typing: boilerplate controllers, standard migrations, repetitive test patterns, routine view templates. This mechanical work doesn't require creativity or judgment—it just requires fingers on keyboards. And it used to require a lot of calendar time.

AI compresses that mechanical work dramatically. Code that took an afternoon to type now takes minutes to generate and review. The bottleneck shifts from production to judgment: deciding what to build, reviewing what AI produces, ensuring quality and security.

A single senior developer can now produce the output volume that previously required a small team. Not by working harder or faster, but by spending their time on decisions rather than typing. The mechanical multiplier that AI provides breaks the link between output volume and team size.

## The New Geometry

When one person can produce team-scale output, the triangle's constraints decouple.

**Cost** drops because you're paying one senior developer instead of five specialists. The coordination overhead that added 30-40% to team projects disappears entirely. Fixed-price engagements become feasible because scope is easier to control with a single point of accountability.

**Speed** increases because there's no coordination latency. No waiting for handoffs, no synchronization meetings, no revision cycles between team members. The person who understands the requirements writes the code, reviews the code, and ships the code.

**Quality** improves because coherence is automatic. A single developer produces consistent patterns, unified architecture, and uniform coding style. There are no interpretation gaps between team members, no conflicts between different technical visions.

The old triangle asked you to pick two. The new geometry lets you optimize all three—not perfectly, not infinitely, but to a degree that would have seemed impossible five years ago.

## The Catch

Nothing is free. AI-augmented development has its own constraints, just different ones from the team model.

The approach only works with genuinely senior developers. AI amplifies skill rather than replacing it. A junior developer using AI produces mediocre code faster; a senior developer produces excellent code faster. The quality dimension depends entirely on who's driving.

Scale eventually requires teams. One developer can build and maintain systems up to a certain complexity. For truly large-scale platforms, you'll eventually need to add people. The question is whether that threshold comes during MVP development or later growth phases.

Specialized depth has limits. A senior generalist can handle most web application requirements. For projects requiring deep expertise in specific domains—complex ML, specialized compliance, unusual integrations—you might need focused specialists.

These constraints matter, but for most MVPs and early-stage products, they don't bite. The one-developer model delivers speed, cost, and quality benefits that more than compensate for its limitations.
